\section*{Context and motivation}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Context and motivation}

Transport supports economic activity by connecting people to jobs and
services, and businesses to suppliers and markets. Reliable transport helps
goods reach their destinations and allows supply chains to function.
The World Bank identifies efficient and affordable transport as an important
contributor to economic growth and employment~\cite{worldbankTransport}.

In Africa, this role is closely linked to regional trade. In a 2022 study,
the \gls{uneca} projected that the
\gls{afcfta} could increase intra-African trade in transport services by
nearly 50\%. The study also highlighted the need for infrastructure to
support the expected growth in freight movements~\cite{uneca2022transport}.
Alongside these infrastructure needs, businesses face a practical question:
how can they make effective use of the vehicles available to them?

Vehicle routing is a recurring responsibility in both private businesses
and public organizations. It is not limited to companies whose main activity
is transport: producers and distributors must also organize the movement of
physical goods. Examples include petroleum distribution, chemicals, food,
agriculture, pharmaceuticals, and waste collection. These activities differ
in their products, customers, and operating conditions, but they share a
planning challenge: assigning work to a limited fleet and choosing the order
in which locations should be served~\cite{toth2014vehicle,wen2010rich}.

A route plan determines more than the roads a vehicle follows. It also
coordinates customer visits, the quantities carried, and the use of the
fleet. A geographically short route may be unsuitable if its load exceeds
vehicle capacity or if the required products are unavailable at the chosen
loading point. In practice, planners must balance these requirements with
cost and service objectives. Vehicle routing research studies how such
decisions can be made together, under different operational
conditions~\cite{lahyani2015taxonomy}.

The planning task becomes more involved when a vehicle performs several
trips. After completing one set of deliveries, it can load again and serve
other customers. Its work therefore consists of a succession of loading,
travel, and delivery operations rather than a single isolated tour.
Multi-trip routing studies examine this repeated use of vehicles and its
consequences for planning~\cite{cattaruzza2016multipletrips}. When successive
loads contain different products, their order introduces a further decision.

For some products, changing a vehicle's load requires cleaning or other
preparation. Consider, as an illustration, a bulk-delivery vehicle that has
just carried a product whose residues are unsuitable for the next load.
Preparing it for that load may incur an additional cost. The preparation
required can depend on both the previous and the next product, and the
reverse transition need not have the same cost. These expenses are referred
to here as \emph{changeover costs}.

This dependence on product order creates a trade-off. Grouping deliveries
of the same product may reduce changeovers but require more travel, whereas
serving nearby customers consecutively may involve several product changes.
The preferred plan depends on the combined costs. This motivates examining
changeovers as part of route planning, so that the cost of preparing a
vehicle is considered alongside the cost of moving it.

\section*{Problem statement}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Problem statement}

Cleaning requirements are already present in routing research. For example,
Lahyani et al. study olive-oil collection with compartment cleaning, while
Tamburini et al. include cleaning in a rich ship-routing and scheduling
model~\cite{lahyani2015oilmulticompartment,TAMBURINI2025104019}. These works
provide important precedents, but their operating settings and preparation
rules differ. The question addressed here is how to represent product
changes between successive trips in the delivery setting defined in this
thesis, and how those costs influence the resulting decisions.

The problem involves stations requiring different products, depots with
available stock, and a fleet that can perform repeated loading and delivery
operations. A plan must satisfy demand, respect stock and vehicle capacity,
and determine how deliveries are assigned and ordered. In addition, the
product previously carried by a vehicle affects the cost of preparing its
next load. We refer to this setting as the \gls{mpvrpcc}. Its objective
combines travel and changeover costs.

These decisions are connected. Changing the order of two trips can alter
both the travel needed between them and the preparation required at the next
loading operation. Assigning a trip to another vehicle can change the
product sequence of both vehicles. Consequently, a plan selected only for
its travel cost may no longer be attractive when its changeovers are
included. Understanding this interaction requires comparing complete plans
under a consistent measure of total cost.

The study investigates \gls{cp} as a way to express these operational rules
and search for feasible plans. It focuses on sequence variables, which
represent the selection and ordering of operations. Previous research has
applied this representation to routing problems~\cite{delecluse2022sequence}.
Here, the aim is to examine its usefulness for MPVRP-CC, including the
representation of product transitions. This requires assessing both the
quality of the solutions obtained and the computational effort needed to
find them.

A \gls{milp} formulation provides a reference for the comparison. Alongside
this comparison of solution methods, the study examines the effect of
including changeover costs in planning. The two evaluations address different
questions: whether the proposed approach performs effectively, and whether
modeling these costs changes the decisions it produces.

The central research question is:
\begin{quote}
\emph{How effectively can CP with sequence variables address MPVRP-CC, and
how does considering changeover costs affect the resulting plans?}
\end{quote}
This question is examined through three specific questions:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item How can the problem's operational constraints and changeover costs
        be represented using CP and sequence variables?
  \item How does the proposed approach compare with a MILP baseline
        in terms of feasible solutions found, total cost, and computation time?
  \item How do vehicle routes, product sequences, and total modeled costs
        change when changeover costs are included in planning?
\end{enumerate}

\section*{Objectives}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Objectives}

The main objective is to develop and assess an approach to MPVRP-CC using CP
and sequence variables, and to examine the effect of changeover costs on
planning decisions. The specific objectives are to:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item define the problem and formulate a MILP reference model;
  \item design and implement a solution approach using CP and sequence variables;
  \item compare the approach with the MILP baseline on synthetic instances; and
  \item evaluate how including changeover costs affects plans and their total cost.
\end{enumerate}

The study uses 100 synthetic instances with varied configurations. The
findings are interpreted within the assumptions and scope of these
experiments. The Methodology chapter explains the choices made as the
approach developed, including the construction of delivery groups before
CP assignment and sequencing.
